From Space to a spoken word

When you press Space, textweaver reads from the cursor. You hear each word, and the same word is highlighted on the screen at the same moment. This page follows one reading through the ten stages that make that happen.

The design is in the architecture guide and in four decision records: ADR-0002: Text model, ADR-0003: Speech threading and event timing, ADR-0005: Narration and the OffsetMap, and ADR-0012: One engine-host protocol and playback client.

The ten stages

The diagram below is a list of the ten stages in order. Each stage is a button: press it to show that stage. The current stage has a thick border and the word "current". Use Next step and Previous step to move one stage at a time, or Show all steps to read every stage at once.

    Step 1 of 10: Document

    The document is canonical text in a rope, plus markers.

    A rope is a tree of text pieces, so edits and lookups stay fast even in a very large book. Markers note what each range of the text is: a heading, a link, a list item, a table, code, or math. Every position is a character position, counted in Unicode scalar values. That keeps saved positions the same as star's. When you press Space, reading starts at the word under the cursor.

    Decided in ADR-0002: Text model.

    Step 2 of 10: Units

    Words and sentences are found with the Unicode text segmentation rules, UAX #29.

    An abbreviation list keeps "Dr." or "e.g." from ending a sentence. A few abbreviations, such as "a.m." and "etc.", end a sentence only when the next word starts with a capital letter. List items, table rows, and code lines are sentences of their own. The same units drive navigation, so "next sentence" and reading aloud always agree.

    Step 3 of 10: Narration

    The narration planner, narrate::plan, turns the text into sentence-sized utterances.

    Continuous reading is planned in windows of about ten minutes of speech (32,768 characters). The next window is planned when this one ends, so a long book starts reading at once.

    The planner adds spoken words for structure that has no text of its own. For example, it says "heading level 2" before a heading, and "list with 3 items" before a list. These are inserted spans: you hear them, but they have no place in the document.

    Decided in ADR-0005: Narration and the OffsetMap.

    Step 4 of 10: Offset map

    An offset map records where every piece of the spoken text came from.

    It is a list of spans. Each span pairs a byte range in the spoken text with a character range in the document. There are four kinds of span:

    • Literal: the same text, character for character.
    • Expanded: spoken words that replace a whole source token, such as "Doctor" for "Dr.". Any word inside highlights the whole token.
    • Inserted: spoken words with no source, such as "heading level 2". They highlight nothing.
    • Elided: source that is not spoken, such as Markdown's **. Its spoken range is empty.

    The spoken text and its map are built together. They are never lined up afterwards by guessing.

    Step 5 of 10: Utterance

    An utterance is what goes to the speech service: the text to speak, an id, and the offset map.

    The id has two parts: the reading generation and the chunk number. The generation changes every time reading stops or restarts. The chunk number counts the utterances within one reading.

    Step 6 of 10: Speech service

    The speech service runs on its own thread, so the screen never waits for speech.

    Its queue keeps two sentences of lookahead in the engine. That way there is no gap between sentences, even with slow engines.

    The service normalizes each utterance on its own, never across two utterances. The steps run in this order: math first, then Markdown residue, then abbreviations, then numbers, then punctuation. Math goes first so that a formula such as $2x$ is not read as money. Every step returns a new offset map, and the maps are chained, so the final map still points into the document.

    Decided in ADR-0003: Speech threading and event timing.

    Step 7 of 10: Backend or engine host

    A backend turns the text into sound, either inside textweaver or in a separate host process.

    Some backends run inside textweaver's own process, such as espeak-ng. Others run in a host process: ETI-Eloquence, SAPI5 voices, and DECtalk. A host loads the engine's library, speaks into memory, and sends the audio and word positions back over a framed pipe protocol. textweaver plays the audio itself. If an engine crashes, only its host stops, and textweaver restarts it.

    Decided in ADR-0012: One engine-host protocol and playback client.

    Step 8 of 10: Word events

    Each word event gives the byte range of a spoken word and the audio time when it is heard.

    For engines that report audio positions, the event is scheduled on the playback clock. So the highlight moves when you hear the word, not when the engine made it. Some engines send no word events, such as Omnivox. For them a timer pacer estimates when each word is heard.

    Step 9 of 10: Generation check

    Events from an older reading generation are dropped, so a late event never moves the highlight.

    Suppose you press Space to pause, then jump to the next heading. The engine may still send an event for a word from before the pause. That event carries the old generation, so the service drops it before anything else sees it.

    Step 10 of 10: Highlight

    The word's byte range maps back through the offset map to a range in the document, and that range is highlighted.

    The terminal reader draws the highlight at once. It also parks the hardware cursor on the spoken word. Screen readers and screen magnifiers follow the hardware cursor, so they follow the reading too.

    Worked example

    Here is one sentence of Markdown with a little math in it. The numbers below come from running tw speak --backend null --json on this sentence.

    The source and the spoken text

    • Source: The area is $x^2$ today. It is 24 characters long, numbered 0 to 23. Every character here is one byte, so byte and character positions are the same in the source.
    • Spoken text: The area is x squared today. It is 28 bytes long.

    A range such as "13 to 14" starts at the first number and stops just before the second, as ranges do in Rust. So "characters 13 to 14" means the one character at position 13.

    The offset map for the example: 7 spans, in spoken order

    How a word maps back

    The math step keeps the letter x as it is. It inserts a space, and it expands the superscript ^2 into the word "squared". The dollar signs are Markdown math delimiters, so they are elided.

    Say the engine reports the word "squared" at spoken bytes 14 to 21. Those bytes fall in the expanded span, and an expanded span always maps to its whole source token. So the highlight covers source characters 14 to 16, which is ^2. The word before it, "x", falls in a literal span and maps to character 13 alone. As you listen to "x squared", the highlight moves from x to ^2, and together the two words cover all of x^2.

    Press Speak next word to step through the spoken words. Nothing is played aloud. The page marks the spoken word and its source range side by side. Each mark is highlighted and has a thick underline, and the text under each box says what is marked.
